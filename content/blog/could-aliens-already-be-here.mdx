The question of whether non-human intelligence is already present in or near Earth is treated as tabloid material. But a careful look at the scientific literature finds that specific, coherent hypotheses have been advanced for exactly this possibility — by researchers working in serious academic contexts.

The zoo hypothesis. Self-replicating probe theory. Directed panspermia. Each proposes a distinct mechanism. None requires faster-than-light travel. None has been proven. All of them deserve a more honest examination than they typically receive.

## The Problem the Galaxy's Age Creates

The Milky Way is old enough that a civilization arising even a few million years before us — a tiny fraction of the galaxy's history — would have had sufficient time to explore the entire galaxy using slow, conventional propulsion. Theoretical physicists and astronomers have modeled this explicitly.

The implication is uncomfortable: if intelligent life is common, the "already here" scenario is actually more statistically expected than a galaxy full of civilizations all staying home and politely broadcasting radio signals in our direction.

## The Tier System Applied

**Tier 1 — Documented fact:** The hypotheses below are real proposals that appear in published scientific and academic literature. The galaxy is genuinely old enough that the scenarios they describe are physically possible under known physics. No confirmed evidence of non-human presence in the solar system or on Earth has been publicly released.

**Tier 2 — Strongly suggested in the aggregate, speculative in specifics:** The Fermi silence — the absence of detectable signals from other civilizations despite statistical expectations — is a real phenomenon that requires explanation. The "already here" class of hypotheses provides a coherent family of explanations for that silence.

**Tier 3 — Speculative:** Any specific claim about who is here, what they want, how the presence manifests, or what evidence would be expected. These are interpretive layers on top of the hypotheses — and those layers have no confirmed foundation.

## Directed Panspermia: The Scientific Proposal

The idea that life on Earth may have been deliberately introduced rather than arising independently was proposed in the scientific literature by Francis Crick — co-discoverer of the structure of DNA — and Leslie Orgel. Their version focused on the origin of life's specific chemistry and the difficulty of explaining it through purely random processes in the time available.

Their proposal was modest: life may have arrived here deliberately rather than arising here independently. It did not include the engineering claim.

The Technospermia framework extends this logic significantly: not merely transport of the seeds of life, but deliberate engineering of organisms — specifically including organisms that produce compounds that interface with consciousness in reproducible ways across species. This is the Tier 3 specific claim. Crick and Orgel's original proposal is the documented scientific precedent.
