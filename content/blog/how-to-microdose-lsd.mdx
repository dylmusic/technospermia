## Why Blinded Studies Show More Modest Effects

The self-report microdosing literature is enthusiastic. Most large-scale surveys find that the majority of participants report improved mood, focus, and creativity. The problem is that most people know whether they are microdosing, which introduces expectation effects.

When blinded — when participants don't know if they received an active dose or placebo — the benefits are smaller and more variable. Several well-designed studies have found that participants who believe they are microdosing (regardless of whether they actually are) report benefits, and those who receive an active dose without knowing show more modest improvements than self-reporters.

This does not mean microdosing does nothing. It means that expectation and attention to positive effects contribute substantially to the reported experience. Knowing this matters for self-assessment: your subjective sense of improvement on a dose day may not be entirely attributable to the compound.

## Dose Guidance

A true microdose is sub-perceptual — you should not notice obvious changes in your perception, mood, or cognition. If you notice you are in an altered state, the dose is too high.

The standard starting point is 5–10 micrograms. This is typically achieved through dilution — making a solution in distilled water and volumetrically dosing, since tabs vary significantly in actual content. Without testing and volumetric dosing, the "same tab, half a tab" approach has unpredictable precision.

LSD is extremely dose-sensitive. The difference between 5 mcg and 15 mcg is meaningful in terms of perceptual effects. Imprecise dosing is the most common reason people report unexpected effects from what they thought was a microdose.

## Tolerance

LSD produces cross-tolerance with other serotonergic psychedelics rapidly. Daily dosing leads to receptor downregulation that reduces effects significantly within a few days. This is why rest days are built into every established protocol — not for safety, but for effectiveness.

A common error is to increase dose when tolerance reduces the felt effect, rather than taking a rest period. Escalating dose to chase the original effect typically pushes beyond the sub-perceptual range and introduces the kind of perceptual shifts that interfere with ordinary function.

## Tracking Your Results Honestly

Keeping a structured log is more valuable than subjective impression. The most common tracking approach: rate mood, focus, energy, and social ease on a 1–10 scale every morning, noting whether it's a dose day or rest day, and reviewing weekly.

This structure separates signal from noise more reliably than asking yourself "does this seem to be helping?" On dose days, you are predisposed to notice positive effects. On rest days, you may be predisposed to notice their absence. Numerical tracking is an imperfect correction for this bias, but it's better than nothing.
