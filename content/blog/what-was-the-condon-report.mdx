The Condon Report was an independent scientific study of UFO evidence commissioned by the U.S. Air Force and conducted by the University of Colorado. Its stated purpose was to evaluate whether continued scientific investigation of UFO reports was warranted. Its conclusion was that it was not.

What followed — the closure of Project Blue Book, the end of formal official investigation for decades — rested on this report as its scientific justification. The legitimacy of that justification has been contested since the report was published.

## What It Was and Why It Was Commissioned

By the time the report was commissioned, Project Blue Book had been running for nearly two decades, had catalogued thousands of cases, and still had a significant residual of unexplained reports. Public pressure and sustained congressional attention to the subject had grown. The Air Force needed a credible scientific assessment to justify either continuing or closing the program.

The University of Colorado was selected to lead the study. The project was led by physicist Edward Condon, a respected scientist with an established reputation. An independent scientific review was intended to provide the Air Force with cover — if a serious university concluded further study was unwarranted, the program could be closed without appearing to simply suppress the question.

## The Tier System Applied

**Tier 1 — Documented fact:** The University of Colorado conducted a federally funded study of UFO evidence led by Edward Condon. A memo — later called the "Low memo" after Robert Low, the project coordinator — described the study as designed to appear scientific while reaching a predetermined conclusion. The memo was real and was eventually obtained and made public, triggering a significant controversy within the committee. Condon expressed skepticism about the subject publicly before the investigation concluded. The final report's own case analyses found a substantial proportion of cases were classified as unexplained.

**Tier 2 — Strongly suggested, unproven:** The Low memo's language — describing how the study could be designed to have "the appearance of a complete scientific study" while being "unbiased" in a way that protects the university's reputation — suggests the study's structure was shaped by institutional conclusions about what it would find before it found it.

**Tier 3 — Speculative:** That the Air Force and the university had agreed in advance on the conclusion. That the cases the committee could not explain represent genuine evidence of non-human phenomena that were intentionally set aside. These interpretations go beyond what the documented record establishes.

## What the Report Found That the Headline Obscured

Within the body of the Condon Report, the scientists who actually worked on the cases encountered a number of cases they described as genuinely puzzling — cases where no conventional explanation was established and the available evidence was difficult to account for through known phenomena.

The report's own scientists described these cases as representing a real residual. The gap between those findings within the report and the headline conclusion at the front of the document is the core of the criticism: the study that was supposed to answer whether UFOs warranted investigation contained evidence within itself that suggested they might, and the summary conclusion did not reflect that evidence proportionally.

## The Legacy

Project Blue Book was closed on the basis of the Condon Report. For decades, official U.S. investigation of UAP was functionally nonexistent. When investigation resumed through programs like AATIP and subsequently the AARO, it represented a de facto acknowledgment that the Condon Report's conclusion — that further study was unlikely to yield useful results — had not been borne out.

The question Blue Book left unexplained, and that the Condon Report recommended setting aside, was eventually reopened under pressure from the accumulation of new evidence and whistleblower testimony. That arc suggests the 1960s closure was a management decision rather than a scientific conclusion.
